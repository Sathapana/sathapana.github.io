Hidden anomalies in a greenhouse climate

Read the data first · white-box anomaly detection · from evaluation to deployment

A fault can leave temperature, humidity and CO₂ each inside their normal band while their combination is impossible. Before building any detector, I read the data: what it measures, how its "normal" is structured, how it drifts. Those findings decided the design — and predicted most of the results.

02. The question

A greenhouse climate is regulated through coupled variables: switching the lamps on heats the air and lowers its relative humidity; opening the vents pulls temperature, humidity and CO₂ towards the outdoor values. Monitoring each variable against its own band misses faults that break these relations — a stuck or drifting probe, a failed aspiration fan, lamps on without the expected warming.

The textbook answer is the Mahalanobis distance, the distance to the centre of a multivariate Gaussian in units of its own covariance:

D²(x) = (x − μ)ᵀ Σ⁻¹ (x − μ)

Its assumptions — one Gaussian regime, clean training data, independent samples — are exactly what the data has to be checked against. The rule throughout is white-box: every alarm explained per variable, every threshold with a stated false-alarm rate.

03. The data, and what it can't tell us

The Autonomous Greenhouse Challenge, 2nd edition (Wageningen University & Research, open data): six glasshouse compartments growing cherry tomatoes for 166 days, five controlled by AI teams and one by commercial growers, logged every 5 minutes — indoor climate, actuators, realised set-points, outdoor weather.

The first finding was a gap. The project was framed around temperature, humidity, light and airflow — but light and airflow are not measured indoors: there is only lamp status, a computed light value and vent openings, and the controller's humidity deficit is just a function of temperature and humidity. So the question changed: not "is any reading abnormal?" but "is the measured climate consistent with what lamps, set-points and weather imply?" Monitored: air temperature, humidity (logit scale, since it is bounded at 100%) and CO₂. Context: the lamp × sun regime, time of day, weather, the realised set-points.

04. What the data revealed

Eight exploratory steps — inventory, quality, distributions, time structure, actuator response, multivariate structure, physics, compartments — each ended with a written conclusion and a decision. Six findings shaped everything that followed.

A fault only a cross-check could see

Seven real sensor faults turned up, all in the growers' compartment: impossible values, a temperature dropout to −1 °C — and this one, which the univariate range and spike rules missed. Only a cross-variable consistency check exposed it: the logged values were not what the controller saw.

So: faulty values are flagged, never deleted — they are the real-fault test set — and the project's core idea (look at variables jointly) was already at work in the cleaning.

"Normal" comes in regimes — and correlations flip sign

The lamp × sun regime alone explains a median 63% of the temperature variance (27% of humidity, 21% of CO₂). Within a regime, tails are heavier than Gaussian: per-regime Gaussian models already alarmed 1.4–3.1% of the time instead of 1%.

So: condition on context rather than fit one ellipse, and read thresholds from the empirical tail rather than from a Gaussian.

The season drifts

Lamps stop after March, growers change strategy, the crop grows: a model trained on the past is miscalibrated in the future for a reason that has nothing to do with anomalies.

So: the main protocol uses a blocked split (seven-day blocks train · train · train · validation · test, every season in every set), and "train on the past, test on the future" was kept as a separate test — it became the deployment section below.

Most of the variance is the schedule; anomalies fade within hours

A distance on the raw values would mostly score the time of day and the season. And because alarms come in runs, the effective sample is only 1–10% of the row count: naive confidence intervals would be about ten times too narrow. Averaging to 30 minutes would hide the real faults, which last 1–4 steps.

So: score residuals, not raw values; keep the 5-minute step; put a one-day buffer around held-out weeks; give every rate a block-bootstrap interval that resamples whole compartment-weeks.

Three variables, no low-dimensional structure

So: PCA-based process control — split the score into a distance inside the main components and a distance from them — has little to split. It stayed in the ladder as a test, with that warning written down.

Each team has its own "normal"

Pooling all compartments hides this: a pooled model looks calibrated only because its covariance is wider, so faults must be 1.15–1.67 times larger to be detected. Calibration has to be read together with sensitivity.

So: one model per compartment.

Guardrails

  • Synthetic anomalies with a null control. Five types injected into the held-out weeks with fixed seeds; every detection rate is an excess over the same windows on clean data, so chance alarms do not count.
  • Interior check (added mid-way, see 08). Spliced injections create jumps at their edges that one-step change detectors react to; detection is also counted only inside the event, away from its edges.
  • A frozen protocol. Splits, injections and metrics were tagged in git before the first method branch; every method faced the same test.
  • Every decision logged. 32 decisions with their rationale and the alternatives rejected, 28 lab-book entries with a falsifiable hypothesis each, negative results kept.

The recommended detector is a multivariate exponentially weighted moving average (MEWMA) chart on the context residual — the measured climate minus its linear prediction from time of day, weather and the exogenous set-points:

e(t) = x(t) − Bᵀ f(c(t))
Z(t) = λ e(t) + (1 − λ) Z(t−1)
T²(t) = Z(t)ᵀ ΣZ⁻¹ Z(t)

Each alarm is explained by reconstruction-based contributions: how much the score drops when one variable is replaced by its best reconstruction from the others.

07. Results: did the data's predictions hold?

  • Attribution works, the T²/SPE split does not — as the structure finding warned. Contributions name the injected variable in 86–91% of detected events.
  • Disease-risk windows. No detector alarms more often during sustained humidity above 90% (a grey-mould risk condition): the exploratory analysis had already shown that humid nights are routine here (12–30% of rows). Without disease records, this is association only.
  • Two checks on the winner. Two of the 55 compartment × set-point pairs turned out to be computed by the controller from the climate itself, which would leak the true state into a synthetic test; the recommended detector uses the exogenous set-points only. Its λ was chosen after seeing test results — so its levels are optimistic — but a bootstrap of the whole selection rule picks it 98% of the time.

08. What the data did not foresee

Reading the data first did not make the results predictable. Three surprises, each of which changed the project:

  • The robust estimator threw away normal operation. Minimum covariance determinant (MCD) estimation is meant to ignore faults hidden in the training data; here it discarded 10–16% of the training rows, concentrated in sunny spring middays. "Normal" is a mixture of regimes, not one ellipse with a few contaminating points.
  • The evaluation itself had a blind spot. One-step change detectors scored well on injected anomalies by reacting to the jumps at their edges, not to the anomalous state. The interior check was added mid-way — documented as a decision, with the earlier results left intact.
  • Refitting does not fix the threshold. Even refitted every week, a threshold read from the training tail is optimistic one week ahead. And the fix — a threshold from recent out-of-sample scores — can be inflated by a single abnormal week.

09. From evaluation to deployment

The drift finding left a debt: the blocked split fits each model on weeks that surround its test weeks, while a deployed detector can only learn from the past. Four checks, each with its hypothesis and decision rule written before it ran, measure the distance.

SettingContext-residual MEWMAVerdict
Never refitted (train Dec–Mar, test late Apr–May)40.6%fails: every detector 7–41%
Fitted on another compartment37% (median)fails: context models do not transfer
Refitted weekly, training-tail threshold7.5%detection kept, calibration lost
Refitted weekly, out-of-sample threshold1.7% (0.6–3.1)near nominal, top group 5/6

Why weekly refitting is not enough. The threshold is read from the residual tail of the very rows the model was just fitted on, and residuals one week ahead are wider: in four compartments, next week's 1% level lies beyond the largest score of the training window. The fix follows from the mechanism — take each week's threshold from the previous four weeks' out-of-sample scores.

Headline. Refitted every week on the last four weeks and alarmed against a threshold from past out-of-sample scores, the context-residual MEWMA is near nominal on the test weeks (1.7% at 1%) and keeps its breadth. It still alarms 2.7% over the season (about three notifications a week), and one abnormal week can inflate its threshold a hundred-fold — enough, in one compartment, to hide the four real faults that followed. The context regression is the robust alternative (0.6%, all four faults caught).

10. What I take from it

  1. Read the data before modelling it. Eight exploratory steps decided the question, the split, the unit of modelling and the uncertainty method — and predicted most of the method outcomes before any method ran.
  2. "Normal" is conditional. The information is in the climate given its exogenous context; most of the work is choosing that context and excluding the variables that react to the anomaly itself.
  3. Calibrate on the statistic's own tail. Gaussian limits — χ², F, the textbook MEWMA covariance (off by 10³–10⁵ here) — fail on real residuals.
  4. Detectors specialise, and an evaluation must be able to tell a change detector from a state detector — hence the interior check.
  5. White-box was not a handicap. Interpretable models matched or beat the black-box benchmarks on every anomaly type.
  6. A threshold is part of the model. Refitting fixes the model, not its calibration; only a threshold estimated out of sample holds in deployment — and that estimator must itself be robust.

11. Caveats, stated up front

  • One season, one crop. Six compartments of one tomato season; the anomalies are mostly synthetic; the seven real faults are all in one compartment.
  • Selected on test data. The recommended λ was chosen after seeing the test results; its levels are optimistic, even though the choice is stable.
  • Deployment numbers are thin. The out-of-sample thresholds need two weeks of past scores, so the deployment check rests on three test weeks and four real faults.
  • The threshold estimator is fragile. A robust version (e.g. the median of weekly quantiles) is deliberately not tuned on these data: it belongs with a confirmatory test.
  • No disease labels. The link to disease risk is association only.

12. Next, and stack

Next: a confirmatory test on unseen data — freeze the deployment setting in a git tag, write the predictions down, then evaluate once on another edition of the challenge (2018, cucumber, same facility) — with a robust out-of-sample threshold tested there, not here.
